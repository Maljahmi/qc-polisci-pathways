Writing well

Papers, policy memos, clear writing, books, videos and AI. Part of Get ready, for graduate school and law school.

Writing gets you into graduate school and law school, wins fellowships and gets you hired. You don't need talent, just a few habits, practice and feedback. This guide covers papers for class, writing for policy, writing clearly, good books and videos, and where AI fits.

Help at Queens College

  • The Writing Center. Kiely Hall 229, 718-997-5676, qc.writing.center@qc.cuny.edu. Book a session of about 50 minutes (walk-ins too) on WCOnline; Friday sessions are online. Tutors help you plan, organize and revise, but they don't edit every sentence for you. It also runs workshops. Writing Center
  • Writing-intensive courses. At QC you take four writing classes: ENGL 110, a College Writing 2 course and two W courses. W courses have at least 5,000 words of graded writing, one revised assignment and no more than 25 students, so they're a good place to get real feedback. W courses
  • Your professors. Bring a draft or an outline to office hours. See Talk to your professors.

Papers for class

  • Start with a question. "Why do some cities have higher turnout?" leads to an argument; "voter turnout" is only a topic.
  • Answer it early. State your argument in the first paragraph, make each paragraph support it, and deal with the strongest point against you.
  • Join the conversation. Summarize what other scholars say before you add your own view.
  • Cite in APSA style. The APSA Style Manual is free online. For a full walk-through, see the UNC Writing Center's guide to political science papers.
  • Revise. Read it aloud, cut what doesn't help the argument, and get one person to read it before you hand it in.

Writing for policy

Policy readers are busy, so the answer comes first: the problem, the options and what you recommend, in plain words and short sections.

  • Policy memos. Harvard Kennedy School's How to Write a Policy Memo (three pages) puts the bottom line up front and weighs options against criteria. Duke's policy memo handout shows the format.
  • Policy briefs. The UNC Writing Center's guide to policy briefs.
  • Op-eds. About 750 to 800 words, a hook and one argument: Harvard Kennedy School's How to Write an Op-Ed.
  • Writing samples. Policy internships and master's programs often ask for one; the Bipartisan Policy Center wants three to five pages. Keep your best memo or paper ready.

Writing clearly

  • Make the actors the subjects and the actions the verbs. "The council cut the budget," not "A reduction of the budget was made by the council."
  • Cut extra words. The UNC Writing Center's Writing Concisely and Purdue OWL's Conciseness show how.
  • Use plain words. Choose "use" over "utilize" and define any term a smart friend outside your class wouldn't know.

Books worth reading

"On reserve" means the Reserve Desk on Level 3 of Rosenthal Library, for short loans. Search the library in CUNY OneSearch.

  • Writing in Political Science: A Practical Guide. Diane E. Schmidt, 5th edition (Routledge, 2018). A step-by-step guide to a political science paper, from picking a topic to using data and citing. Routledge
  • "They Say / I Say." Gerald Graff and Cathy Birkenstein, 6th edition (Norton, 2024). Templates for summarizing others and entering a debate. An older edition is on reserve. Norton
  • The Craft of Research. Wayne C. Booth and others, 5th edition (University of Chicago Press, 2024). Turning a topic into a question, an argument and evidence, with a note on using AI. Free as an e-book through the QC library with your CUNY login. Chicago
  • The Elements of Style. William Strunk Jr. and E. B. White, 4th edition. Short, firm rules for concise sentences; on reserve, and Strunk's original 1920 text is free on Project Gutenberg.
  • Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace. Joseph M. Williams and Joseph Bizup, 13th edition (Pearson, 2021). How to revise sentences so readers follow them. Pearson
  • The Sense of Style. Steven Pinker (Penguin, 2015). Why expert writing gets murky and how to fix it; on reserve. Penguin Random House
  • Writing for Social Scientists. Howard S. Becker, 3rd edition (University of Chicago Press, 2020). Writer's block, jargon and finishing a thesis. Chicago
  • For policy. A Practical Guide for Policy Analysis by Eugene Bardach and Eric M. Patashnik (7th edition, 2023; the 5th is on reserve), and Writing Public Policy by Catherine F. Smith (6th edition, Oxford, 2022) for memos, briefs and testimony.

Using AI in your writing

  • Know the rule. Under CUNY's Academic Integrity Policy, using AI to produce work for an assignment without your instructor's written permission is cheating, and using AI-generated content without citing it is plagiarism. Each professor sets the rules for their class, so read the syllabus and ask. QC's teaching center explains this on its AI resources page.
  • If it's allowed. Use it to brainstorm, to question your outline or to check grammar, and write the words yourself. Check every fact and citation, because AI invents sources, and keep private information out. The UNC Writing Center's guide to AI in academic writing goes further.
