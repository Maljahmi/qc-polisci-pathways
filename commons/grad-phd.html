What a PhD is

Original research and teaching. A PhD prepares you to be a professor, or to do research at think tanks, in government and in tech. Political science PhD programs pay you.

  • Length: 5–6 years
  • Who pays: The school: tuition plus a stipend. 2026–27: Princeton $52,680, Duke $43,775, CUNY Graduate Center $31,141
  • Pick it if: You want answering questions with data or theory to be your career
  • Apply: Fall of senior year or later. Most deadlines fall Dec 1–17; Columbia's is Nov 25. See Applying

A political science PhD can also lead to data science. PhD students train in statistics, causal inference, survey design and coding, and some go straight into industry. Michigan's placement records include a research scientist on Meta's demography and survey science team, a director of data science at Civiqs and a lead data scientist at Cox Automotive. MIT's include a data scientist at NORC. Choosing methodology as a minor field keeps that door open.

A master's is not required for a PhD. Most political science PhD students are admitted straight from a BA or after a paid research job. A paid year or two first is normal; the standard route is a predoc, a full-time research assistant job.

Where to look. predoc.org is the main job board; filter by political economy or public policy. Listings go stale, so confirm on the lab's own page. For a pay benchmark, Stanford's SIEPR economics predoc posted $64,260 a year.

Find your subfield

You don't have to be a "methods person" to get a political science PhD. You apply to study one subfield, only some subfields are math-heavy, and most programs teach statistics from the start.

The five core subfields

American politics

Math: Mixed · often quantitative

Also called American government and politics

U.S. institutions such as Congress, the presidency and the courts, plus elections, public opinion and political behavior.

  • Look for: No special prerequisites are listed. Expect statistics: Harvard's math prefresher notes it is hard to take American politics courses without meeting statistical or game-theoretic models

Comparative politics

Math: Mixed · qualitative and quantitative

Compares politics across countries and regions: democracy, development, civil war, institutions and identity.

  • Look for: A foreign language and knowledge of a region. Rutgers tests majors in the language they need, and Michigan advises starting languages before graduate school
  • Research: Berkeley PhD students usually spend at least a year doing fieldwork

International relations

Math: Mixed · depends on approach

Also called international relations and world politics

War and security, trade, international law and organizations, and why countries cooperate or fight.

  • Look for: Methods vary: historical, statistical and game-theoretic work all appear. No IR-specific prerequisites are listed

Political theory

Math: Light · little math

Also called political thought, or normative political theory and philosophy

Ideas of justice, legitimacy, democracy and power, read through the history of political thought and contemporary philosophy.

  • Look for: Reading in philosophy, history or classics, and often a foreign language: UVA requires one for theory majors
  • Methods: Duke's methods requirements don't apply to theory students, Harvard lets them skip its methods course, and Berkeley weighs math less for theory applicants

Methodology and formal theory

Math: Heavy · real math

Also called research methods, quantitative methodology, or formal and quantitative analysis

Methodologists build the statistical and research-design tools other political scientists use. Formal theorists use game-theory models of strategic choices.

  • Look for: Math and statistics. Duke's methodology field expects competency in both, and Michigan asks methods majors for math coursework
  • Tip: Many students take methods as a minor next to a substantive subfield

You don't have to arrive methods-heavy

  • Math camps. Harvard, Stanford, Michigan, Princeton and UC San Diego run summer math courses for incoming PhD students. At Harvard, almost every first-year student takes it.
  • Methods from scratch. Stanford says you don't need advanced quantitative courses to be admitted, and its first-year sequence assumes no background in statistics or advanced math.
  • Language or methods. At the CUNY Graduate Center, UCLA, Yale and Columbia, a research-tools requirement can be met with a foreign language or with methods courses.
  • Your topic can change. Michigan notes that students often change focus once coursework starts. The statement of purpose is not a contract.

Be honest with yourself about math. Quantitative skills still count. Most top programs require the GRE again, and Stanford reports admitted students typically score 163 or higher on the quantitative section. Stanford also requires two methods courses of every PhD student. Taking PSCI 200 and an intro statistics course, such as PSYCH 107 or DATA 205, helps whichever subfield you choose.

Other fields at some schools

Some departments add fields beyond the core five. Check whether a field can be your main field or only a minor.

  • Public policy. CUNY Graduate Center (Public Policy, with areas like health, education, housing and urban development), UC Berkeley (Public Policy & Organization), UT Austin, Texas A&M (Public Policy / Public Administration), Syracuse (public administration and policy) and Michigan (a student-designed Public Policy and Administration minor).
  • Law and courts. Princeton and Rutgers (Public Law), Michigan (Law, Courts, and Politics), UC Berkeley (Public Law & Jurisprudence), Washington and UT Austin, which also has a combined JD/PhD.
  • Race, ethnicity and gender. UCLA (Race, Ethnicity & Politics), Rutgers (Women and Politics), UC Berkeley, Washington and Ohio State (Race, Ethnicity, and Gender).
  • Political psychology and behavior. Stony Brook, Ohio State and Minnesota (as a minor).
  • Political economy and formal theory. Rochester (formal political theory; every student takes it or methodology), Yale, NYU and UChicago, which runs a separate Political Economy PhD with its public policy school. Expect heavy math.
  • Organized by theme. Duke has no American, comparative or international relations fields. Its six fields are Political Behavior & Identities, Political Institutions, Political Economy, Security, Peace & Conflict, Political Methodology, and Normative Political Theory & Philosophy.
