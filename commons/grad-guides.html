Start here

This path covers funded PhDs and policy master's for any Queens College Political Science major who is even curious about graduate school. You don't need to be sure yet. Most of these programs exist to help you find out.

Three ground rules

  • Good research programs pay you. Almost everything here is free and comes with a stipend, housing, or both. Be skeptical of programs that charge you to "do research."
  • Deadlines move every year. Confirm on the official page before you plan around a date.
  • Apply to more than one. These programs are competitive, and a rejection says little about you.
  • Take the methods courses early. PSCI 200 (Research Skills) and an intro statistics course such as PSYCH 107 or DATA 205. Either one also counts toward the Data Analytics minor.
  • Ask for research assistant work. No office posts it. Pick one or two professors whose work you understand, and email what you read, what skill you bring and how many hours you have.

Get credit for it. QC's political science internship courses give credit for urban politics, law and advocacy, and international politics placements. CUNY's Rogowsky Internship Program places juniors and seniors in government for 3–12 credits.

Undocumented or an international student? Emory SOAR says it accepts undocumented and international students. MIT MSRP accepts international students with a visa and work authorization. RBSI, MIT and Michigan also take DACA recipients.

Glossary

Graduate school and law school come with a lot of terms nobody explains. Here is what they mean, in plain English.

Degrees and programs

  • PhD: A research doctorate, usually five to six years in political science. Most programs pay your tuition and a living stipend while you research and teach. More about the PhD
  • Predoc: A paid, full-time research job for one or two years, usually at a university, that prepares you for a PhD. More about predocs
  • MPP: Master of Public Policy. About two years; trains you to design and evaluate policy. More about the MPP
  • MPA: Master of Public Administration. One to two years; trains you to run government and nonprofit organizations. More about the MPA
  • MA: Master of Arts. One to two years of graduate study in one subject, often without funding. More about the MA
  • JD: Juris Doctor, the three-year law degree you need to become a lawyer in the United States. Law school
  • Pipeline program: A free program that helps students from groups underrepresented in a field get in, often with test prep and mentors. Pre-law pipeline programs
  • Fellowship: Money or a paid position, usually competitive, for study, research or work. Some are jobs and some are scholarships.

Applying

  • Application cycle: The yearly round of applications. Most graduate and law programs open in late summer or fall for the next fall.
  • Rolling admissions: Decisions are made as applications arrive, so applying early can help. Most law schools work this way.
  • Early decision: An early application that is binding: if you're admitted, you agree to attend. Early action is early but not binding.
  • Statement of purpose: The essay for graduate programs about what you want to study and why. Law schools ask for a personal statement instead, about who you are. Statements
  • Writing sample: A paper you wrote, usually a research paper, that shows how you write and think. The writing sample
  • Letter of recommendation: A letter a professor or supervisor writes about you. Most programs want two or three. Recommendation letters
  • CV: Curriculum vitae: an academic résumé that lists your education, research and presentations. Sample résumé and CV
  • Transcript: The record of your courses and grades. Many programs accept an unofficial copy when you apply and ask for the official one later.
  • GRE: A standardized test some graduate programs require. Many PhD and policy programs no longer do, so check each one. Applying to PhD programs
  • Fee waiver: Permission to apply without paying the application fee. Many schools offer them, and so does LSAC for law school. Fee waivers
  • Waitlist: A list of applicants who may be admitted if spots open up later.

Law school

  • LSAT: The Law School Admission Test. Law schools weigh it heavily, along with your GPA. LSAT and fees
  • LSAC: The Law School Admission Council, which runs the LSAT and the system you use to apply to law schools.
  • CAS: LSAC's Credential Assembly Service, which collects your transcripts and letters and sends them to each law school. It has a fee unless you get an LSAC fee waiver.
  • ABA: The American Bar Association, which accredits law schools in the United States.
  • 1L: A first-year law student. 2L and 3L are the second and third years.
  • Deferral program: A way to apply to law school while still in college and start after working for a few years, like Harvard's Junior Deferral Program.

Money

  • Funded: The program pays your tuition and gives you a living stipend. Most political science PhDs are funded; most master's and law degrees are not. Scholarships and funding
  • Stipend: Money paid to you for living costs by a program, fellowship or internship.
  • FAFSA: The Free Application for Federal Student Aid, which you file to get federal grants, loans and work-study. studentaid.gov
  • Pell Grant: Federal grant money for undergraduates with financial need. You don't pay it back.
  • Federal Work-Study: A part-time job, often on campus, paid partly through your financial aid.

At Queens College

  • Office hours: Times professors set aside to meet students. You don't need an appointment or a problem to go. Talk to your professors
  • Research assistant: A student who helps a professor with research, sometimes paid or for credit. Often called an RA.
  • Honors thesis: A year-long research paper written with a faculty adviser, like Queens College's Honors in the Social Sciences.
  • Summer research program: A paid summer doing research with a professor at another university, often with housing. Good preparation for a PhD. Summer research programs

Talk to your professors

Your research doesn't have to be political science. Methods travel across fields, and CUNY faculty in many departments need student help with surveys, data, experiments, fieldwork and archives.

What research looks like next door

  • Sociology. Surveys and interviews on inequality, immigration, work and neighborhoods. Home of QC's data analytics program.
  • Economics. Labor markets and policy evaluation, often with large administrative datasets and causal inference.
  • Psychology. Experiments on attitudes, prejudice and decision-making, the base of political psychology.
  • Urban studies. Housing, transit, policing and city budgets, often with NYC open data.
  • Anthropology and history. Fieldwork, oral histories and archives.
  • Linguistics. Text as data, and language and identity (see Pair your major).

Going to office hours

Office hours are not only for grade questions. Professors expect students to come in and ask for advice, and most of the opportunities on this site started with a conversation like that.

  • Go to office hours or email to ask for 15 minutes. It's normal, even if you've never taken their class.
  • Ask for advice, not a favor: "What would you do in my position?" "Which classes or programs should I look at?" "Who else should I talk to?"
  • Ask about their research, and read one of their papers first.
  • Look outside your department. Sociology, economics, psychology and urban studies faculty take political science students too.
  • Follow up. Send a thank-you, then tell them what you did with their advice. That is how a professor becomes a recommender.

Sample email

Subject: Advice on research opportunities, [Your name], [major]

Dear Professor [Last name],

I'm a [year] majoring in [major] at Queens College. I read your [paper or project] on [topic], and I'm interested in [one sentence on why].

I'm hoping to get research experience before applying to [PhD or master's programs]. Could I come to your office hours on [day] to ask for advice on next steps? If you ever need help on a project, I can offer [a skill, e.g., R or survey coding] for [number] hours a week.

Thank you,
[Full name]

Pair your major

Take a broad range of courses early. Trying history, philosophy, economics or urban studies is how you find the questions you actually want to study, and a second field can make you a stronger applicant.

Theory, history and cities

Philosophy

Minor (18 credits) or second major

Arguments about justice, rights and legitimacy, the core of political theory. The minor includes two history-of-philosophy courses (PHIL 140–148). Best for students drawn to political theory.

History

Minor or second major

Primary sources, archives and long-run change. Useful for political theory, comparative politics and the history of American institutions.

Urban Studies

Major, or a BA and MA in five years

City politics, housing, policing and budgets, often with NYC data. The Accelerated MA pairs a BA in Urban Studies with an MA in Urban Affairs in five years, and up to 12 graduate credits count toward both. It needs a 3.0 GPA overall and in the major; majors in other social sciences are considered case by case. Apply at about 45–75 credits. Best for city government and MPA programs.

Sociology

Double major

Survey research and theory; home of Data Analytics. It unlocks the 4+1 BA/MA in data analytics and Mellon Mays, neither of which takes political science alone.

Data, economics and business

A quantitative credential helps for policy, campaign, government and research jobs. The Bureau of Labor Statistics projects data scientist jobs to grow 35% from 2025 to 2035 (BLS).

Data Analytics

Minor (21 credits), in Sociology

The easiest add-on for most political science majors, built for students with no background. Courses: DATA 205 (or PSYCH 107 or ECON 249), 212W, 235, 306, 333 and 334 (list). DATA 205, 306 and 333 teach R. Best for analyst and research assistant jobs, predocs and MPP programs.

Math: Data Science and Statistics

BA, as a second major

Statistics, programming and modeling. Best for methods-heavy PhDs and data science jobs.

Economics

Major or minor

Policy analysis and econometrics. Best for MPPs, economics predocs and think tanks. For more advanced methods, the Quantitative Economics BS adds calculus, linear algebra, advanced econometrics and scientific computing, and is a STEM major.

Computer Science

Minors

Also Computer Information Technology. Best for computational social science and tech policy.

Text as data

Courses, a minor or a BS in Linguistics and Communication Disorders

Want to analyze speeches, bills or social media posts with code? The department teaches LCD 151 and 251, Methods in Computational Linguistics I and II. Go further with the minor (built for CS and linguistics majors; 33 credits for other majors) or the BS in Computational Linguistics.

Business and Liberal Arts (BALA)

Honors minor (21 credits), open to all majors

Business concepts, communication and analytical decision-making. Best for consulting, government management and MPA programs.

Skills employers look for, whatever you pair it with

  • A statistical language. R or Python, plus Excel. SQL if you want data jobs.
  • Survey and research design. Writing a questionnaire, sampling, reading a regression table.
  • Short-form writing. Policy memos and one-page briefs, not only seminar papers.
  • Proof. A GitHub page with one or two finished projects. See the samples.

Methods and skills

Queens College already teaches solid research methods in PSCI 200 and in intro statistics courses like PSYCH 107 and DATA 205. Several teach R, including DATA 205, DATA 306, DATA 333 and Professor Earl Asberry's section of PSCI 200. If you want advanced training, the funded summer research programs like RBSI and MIT MSRP teach graduate-level methods. They are just as good as a summer internship, if not better, and some pay more. Outside class, you already have free access to LinkedIn Learning.

Free self-study, in order

  1. Khan Academy, Statistics and probability. A free online course with short videos and practice problems. Start here if you have never taken statistics.
  2. OpenIntro Statistics. Probability, inference and regression from scratch (free PDF).
  3. R for Data Science. Cleaning, plotting and summarizing data in R.
  4. MIT 17.801, Political Science Scope and Methods. How political scientists design research.
  5. Blackwell, A User's Guide to Statistical Inference and Regression. Regression, done carefully. Read it once you have learned the basics of statistics.
  6. Research Design in the Social Sciences and Causal Inference: The Remix. Causal inference, once regression feels comfortable.

Statements, help and AI

Pick programs that fit, and get help with your statement. Read what each program focuses on and make sure it matches what you want to study. Then ask your professors and Rebecca Baron, QC's Advisor for Scholarships and Fellowships in the Office of Honors and Scholarships, to help with your statements of purpose. They are here to help. Don't lose out on an opportunity because you were scared to ask. You're in college to learn, so learn.

Don't let AI write your application. AI is useful for some tasks, like building this website. But professors read the applications to these programs, and they will know if AI wrote yours. Use it as a proofreader instead: give it the prompt and your own draft, and ask where you could improve or show more of yourself. It's a machine, and it doesn't know you better than you know yourself. Before you submit, have peers and professors read your writing too.

Applying

Political science PhD applications are due from late November to December 17 for fall entry (Columbia's is Nov 25), and the APSA Diversity Fellowship closes earlier, on October 25, 2026.

Application fees ($75–$125 each) and who waives them

Program not on the list? Email the department's graduate coordinator and ask anyway, in October rather than the week of the deadline.

The GRE is back at most top programs

  • Required for fall 2027. Harvard (Government), Princeton, Stanford, Yale, Duke, Berkeley, Columbia, MIT, UChicago, UCLA.
  • Not required. Michigan, Wisconsin, Northwestern, CUNY Graduate Center.
  • Plan. Check each program page, and test by early fall of senior year.

The rest of the file

  • Letters (usually 3). From faculty who have seen your research or writing. Ask 6–8 weeks ahead: see Recommendation letters.
  • Statement of purpose. Lead with the question you want to study, show your preparation, and name 2–3 faculty per school whose work fits.
  • Writing sample. Your best research paper, often a seminar paper or thesis chapter, cut to the page limit and revised with a professor.
  • Where to apply. Fit with faculty matters more than rank. Look for people whose recent papers you would want to have written.

Recommendation letters

Most programs ask for two or three letters. PhD programs want them from professors. For law school, MPA and MPP programs and jobs, a supervisor from a job or internship can write one too.

Not close with any professors or employers?

Most students aren't at first. Professors write letters all the time, and they say yes when you make it easy for them.

  • Pick a class you did well in, ideally one where you got a B or above and participated.
  • Ask to meet. Look up their office hours, or email to ask when you could meet on Zoom.
  • Ask 6–8 weeks before the deadline, not the week of.
  • Send everything in one email: your résumé, a draft of your statement and your list of programs with deadlines.
  • Remind and thank. Send a short reminder a week before the deadline, and tell them where you got in.

PhD visit programs

Many universities invite prospective PhD applicants to campus in the fall to meet faculty and graduate students and learn how to apply. Most pay for travel, a hotel and meals, and some waive the application fee. Most fly-ins take applications in July and August, so apply the summer before senior year.

Every visit program is in the PhD opportunities list. Open now: Notre Dame's Our Futures Weekend (juniors, due Nov 15) and Princeton's P3 (due Mar 15).

No visit? You can still get a fee waiver. Yale waives its fee for Pell Grant recipients, and UChicago Social Sciences waives it for undergrads on need-based aid. See Applying for more.

Dual degrees

A dual degree pairs law school with a master's, like a JD and an MPP, so you finish both in about four years instead of five. You almost always apply to each school separately.

Is it worth it? A dual degree helps when the job you want needs both, like policy work at a civil rights group or international law. It also adds a year of tuition and time, so most people only do one once they know they want both. You can usually add the second school during your first year of law school, so you don't have to decide before you start.

Paying for graduate and law school

A political science PhD is usually funded: the program pays your tuition and a salary. Master's degrees and law school usually are not, and federal loan rules changed on July 1, 2026. Here is what that means, and how to pay less.

Funded and unfunded

  • PhD programs. Most pay tuition and a living salary for five or six years in return for research and teaching. The CUNY Graduate Center's fellowship, for example, is $31,141 a year for five years plus tuition. See Scholarships and funding.
  • Master's and law school. Most students pay with a mix of the school's scholarships, outside scholarships and loans, so apply for the school's aid and compare offers. Big outside awards include the Paul & Daisy Soros Fellowships (up to $90,000 for immigrants and children of immigrants) and the ABA Legal Opportunity Scholarship ($15,000 over law school, applications January 15 to March 15, 2027).

Federal loans since July 1, 2026

  • File the FAFSA. In graduate school you count as independent, so your parents don't add their information. Graduate students can get Direct Unsubsidized Loans; subsidized loans are only for undergraduates. studentaid.gov
  • Grad PLUS is gone for new borrowers. In the Department of Education's words: "It's no longer possible for you to receive a Direct PLUS Loan for graduate or professional students." Students already enrolled in June 2026 with a loan can keep it for that program for a few more years.
  • The interest rate. 8.07% fixed for graduate unsubsidized loans first paid out in 2026–27, plus a 1.057% fee. Rates
  • Repaying. New loans now go into the Repayment Assistance Plan, where payments are 1% to 10% of your income ($50 less a month per dependent), or a Tiered Standard Plan.

From studentaid.gov's loan limit rules, updated August 24, 2026. A federal court paused part of the "professional degree" definition in June 2026, so ask each school's financial aid office how your program counts. Schools can set lower limits.

Public Service Loan Forgiveness

  • What it is. PSLF forgives what is left on your federal Direct Loans after 120 qualifying monthly payments (ten years) while you work full time, 30 hours a week or more, for government, a 501(c)(3) nonprofit or some other nonprofits. AmeriCorps and Peace Corps service count. Companies, labor unions and partisan political groups don't. PSLF
  • The new rules. Payments under the new Tiered Standard Plan don't count. Under the Repayment Assistance Plan, for loans made after July 1, 2026, only full, on-time payments count. A 2025 rule that would have excluded some employers was thrown out by a federal judge on June 30, 2026.
  • Use the tool. The PSLF Help Tool checks whether an employer qualifies and tracks your payments.

Law school loan repayment help

Many law schools help graduates in public-interest or government jobs repay their loans, through a Loan Repayment Assistance Program (LRAP). The terms differ a lot, so ask every law school that admits you. Three examples:

  • NYU Law. For the Class of 2029, graduates using PSLF pay nothing toward their loans until they earn $110,000, and the program now covers private loans too. NYU LRAP
  • Columbia Law. Graduates in public-interest jobs pay 30% of income over $70,000 ($110,000 under its PSLF option), and the help is forgiven over years of service. Columbia LRAP
  • Fordham Law. Up to $8,900 a year for legal jobs at nonprofits paying $73,000 or less (Class of 2025 figures); government jobs don't qualify. Fordham LRAP

Ways to pay less

  • Go to CUNY. For New York residents, a CUNY master's is $5,545 a semester, an MPA $6,375 and CUNY Law $7,725. CUNY tuition
  • Serve first. A year with AmeriCorps, like City Year or NYC Civic Corps, earns a $7,395 Segal Education Award for school or loans, and counts toward PSLF.
  • Work for the city. Full-time New York City employees can apply for the Mayor's Graduate Scholarship, which participating colleges fund.
  • Skip the fees. Ask for application fee waivers; see Applying to PhD programs and LSAT and fees.

Loan rules changed in 2025 and 2026 and may change again. Check studentaid.gov before you borrow.

Scholarships and funding

Queens College has its own scholarship money, and one application each semester puts you in the running for most of it. Start there, then work with the Office of Honors and Scholarships on national awards. Every scholarship below is also in the opportunities lists with its deadline.

At Queens College

  1. Fill out the General Application every semester. One application on QC's AcademicWorks portal covers most QC Foundation scholarships, and the office says most are open to students without documentation. This fall's closes October 25, 2026; the spring cycle runs from mid-January to mid-March.
  2. Answer the supplemental questions. Some awards ask one more short question at the end, like the Ottensoser Award, which helps juniors and seniors pay graduate school application fees and test costs.
  3. Let the department know you. The Political Science department's awards have no application. Professors choose.
  4. Graduating with a 3.75 or higher? Seniors with a 3.75+ GPA and 45 QC credits get an email in the spring to be considered for the Baccalaureate Award Ceremony's named awards, including several for students headed to law school or public-interest careers. Fill out the survey in that email.
  5. Get paid for an unpaid internship. The QC Internship Stipend Fund gives $2,700 once. With a work-study award, the NYC Public Service Corps pays you to intern in government.

National awards, by year

The Office of Honors and Scholarships advises on national awards. For some, Queens College has to nominate you, and its own deadline comes before the national one, so start early.

  • Freshmen and sophomores. JK Watson Fellowship (QC nominates), Fulbright UK Summer Institutes, Critical Language Scholarship (Nov 17) and, for study abroad, Gilman.
  • Juniors. Truman, Beinecke (QC nominates), Boren and the Rangel summer program. Plan now for Rhodes, Marshall and Schwarzman: their applications are due early in senior year.
  • Seniors. Fulbright (apply through QC), Carnegie Junior Fellows (QC nominates), the Huntington Public Service Award and, for future teachers, the James Madison Fellowship.
  • Check every year. Belle Zeller (30 CUNY credits and a 3.75+ GPA), Pilsbury (first-generation), APIA Scholars and the Hispanic Scholarship Fund.

Programs that pay for housing. A summer away from home costs much less when the program houses you. With free housing and a stipend: the Hudson Institute and Hertog political studies programs ($3,000 each), Hertog's security, war and constitutional studies programs, the Publius Fellowship, and research programs like RBSI and PPIA. AEI and Hoover cover housing for their summer courses. Washington internships with housing: CHCI, CBCF, APAICS, Victory, CHLI, the Islamic Scholarship Fund, the Muslim Public Service Network, AAPD (for students with disabilities) and the Institute for Responsible Citizenship. Pre-law: USC's ASCEND and Florida State's summer program. After college, AmeriCorps NCCC, the Jesuit Volunteer Corps and the Peace Corps house you while you serve. Search every program for housing.

Study or work abroad after college. For a fully funded master's abroad, look at Rhodes and Marshall (QC must endorse you; due around October 1 of senior year), Gates Cambridge, Oxford's Clarendon, the Yenching Academy and Schwarzman in Beijing, Erasmus Mundus in Europe and DAAD in Germany. Paid teaching jobs include Fulbright English teaching assistantships, JET in Japan, TAPIF in France, language assistants in Spain and EPIK in Korea. For a service year, try Princeton in Asia, Africa or Latin America, the Luce Scholars or the Peace Corps. Every one is in All opportunities.

Muslim and Arab American students. Queens College has a large Muslim community, and several national programs are built for it. The Islamic Scholarship Fund gives $3,000–10,000 scholarships to Muslim juniors, seniors and graduate students in political science, policy, journalism and law, and runs paid congressional and newsroom internships (due Nov 1). For a summer in Washington, look at MPAC's Hill program and the Muslim Public Service Network. Closer to home, CAIR-NY in Manhattan and Emgage in Queens take students. Arab American students can apply to the Arab American Leadership Program (Dec 13) and the Arab American Institute.

Not a US citizen? Many national awards require citizenship, but not all. Most of QC's own scholarships are open to students without documentation. TheDream.US is for undocumented students, and Queens College is a partner school. The Hispanic Scholarship Fund, the Fund for Education Abroad and RBSI accept DACA recipients. Ask the Office of Honors and Scholarships about the rest.

Journalism, nonprofits and government

Not every path goes straight to graduate school. These programs pay you to report, organize or work in government, and many take students from any year.

  • Journalism. Paid newsroom internships, like POLITICO's in New York (Oct 26), and fellowships after college, like The Atlantic's (Nov 5). QC students can start CUNY's journalism master's in senior year through the Newmark 4+1. All journalism programs.
  • Nonprofits. A paid summer at a Brooklyn nonprofit through the Hope Reichbach Fellowship, a semester in Albany with NYPIRG, or a service year after college with City Year, Teach For America or NYC Civic Corps.
  • Government. The Mayor's Office, the State Attorney General, congressional offices including Rep. Grace Meng's in Queens, and federal agencies. The CIA, FBI and State Department hire 6–15 months ahead and require US citizenship.

Looking for more after college? ProFellow's free list of public service fellowships for recent graduates has dozens more, like the Carnegie Junior Fellows (QC nominates) and the CHCI and APAICS fellowships on Capitol Hill. Its full database needs a free account. Some details on ProFellow are out of date, so confirm on each program's official page.

International affairs and the Foreign Service

International affairs careers run through the Foreign Service, international organizations like the United Nations, think tanks, nonprofits and international business. Most people get there with a language, time abroad, an internship or two and, often, a master's degree. A lot changed in 2025, so this guide uses official pages checked in October 2026.

At Queens College

  • The International Politics track. An optional track in the political science major: PSCI 103, 104 and one more 100-level course, PSCI 200, two courses from PSCI 230 to 269 (like International Organization, U.S. Foreign Policy, Politics of U.S. National Security or International Political Economy), a seminar and three more electives.
  • Model UN. PSCI 297 is a 3-credit Model United Nations course that includes a week-long conference in New York with sessions at UN headquarters. The Model United Nations club goes to two National Model UN conferences a year.
  • Internships for credit. PSCI 2964, Internship in International Politics, and PSCI 299, a summer internship in Washington. See political science internships.
  • A language. The Foreign Service doesn't require one, but fellowships like Boren and the Critical Language Scholarship reward it. QC teaches Arabic, Chinese, Japanese, Korean and Hebrew (Classical, Middle Eastern and Asian Languages), French, German, Italian and Russian (European Languages), and Spanish and Portuguese (Hispanic Languages).
  • A minor or a region. The Global Studies minor (18 credits, a language course and an intercultural experience), or a program on a region, like Middle Eastern Studies, East Asian Studies, Latin American Area Studies, Africana Studies, Jewish Studies or Russian. QC has no international studies major; City College does.
  • Time abroad. The Study Abroad Office (718-997-5050) runs exchanges like CUNY-Paris and CUNY-Italy and lists scholarships.

The Foreign Service

  • How you get in now. Pass the Foreign Service Officer Test (FSOT), then an oral assessment, then medical and security clearances and a final review. The test was revamped in October 2025: it added a logic and reasoning section and dropped the personal essays. In 2026 it is given every quarter, and you register on Pearson VUE about a month before each window. How the process works.
  • Who can apply. U.S. citizens who are at least 20 and under 60 when they register. No specific degree or foreign language is required. You can take the test once in 12 months, so try the practice test first.
  • Foreign Service Specialists. 17 career tracks, from management and technology to security and health, with their own test and interview.
  • Consular Fellows. If you speak Arabic, Mandarin Chinese, Portuguese or Spanish, you can work abroad for up to five years deciding visa cases. It does not give you a faster way into the Foreign Service. Consular Fellows.
  • Paid internships. The State Department student internship pays at the GS-4 level and provides housing. You need 60 credits and a 3.2 GPA, and summer internships are usually filled from applications made the October before. The 2027 cycle has closed.
  • Two fellowships are on hold. The Rangel and Pickering graduate fellowships, which paid for a master's in exchange for joining the Foreign Service, postponed their 2026 cycles "pending updates from the U.S. Department of State." The Rangel summer program for undergraduates is still running; its 2027 application opens in November 2026.

International organizations

  • UN internships. Unpaid, two to six months, many at headquarters in Manhattan. Open to students in the last year of a bachelor's degree, graduate students and recent graduates. Each office posts its own on careers.un.org, open for about two or three weeks, so check often.
  • UN Young Professionals Programme. The UN's entry exam for a career at the UN. U.S. citizens 32 or younger with a bachelor's degree can apply for the 2026 round from October 16 to November 30, 2026, with the exam around February 2027. Young Professionals Programme.
  • World Bank and UNICEF. The World Bank's paid internship, now called WBG Pioneers, takes final-year undergraduates and graduate students, with windows in January to February and July to August. UNICEF internships pay a monthly stipend and take undergraduates and recent graduates.
  • The IMF. The Research Analyst Program is a two-year job for new graduates in economics or a quantitative field with a 3.5 GPA.

Master's degrees in international affairs

Most international affairs careers ask for a master's eventually. A Master of International Affairs (MIA) or an MA in international relations centers on foreign policy, security, development and international organizations; an MPP or MPA centers on designing and running policy. Many schools offer both. Tuition below is for 2026–27 where the school posts it.

The Association of Professional Schools of International Affairs lists 128 fellowships for these programs, filterable for multicultural students, and runs free graduate school fairs, including an online fair on October 29, 2026.

The cheapest route is CUNY. City College's and Baruch's international affairs master's cost about $6,375 a semester for New York residents, against $60,000 to $75,000 a year at most private schools. Princeton is the exception: it covers tuition for every master's student.

Paying for a language and time abroad

  • Critical Language Scholarship. A free summer of language study abroad in nine languages, due November 17, 2026. It took about 315 students in 2026, down from nearly 600, so apply early and widely. Critical Language Scholarship.
  • Boren. Up to $25,000 to study a language abroad outside Western Europe, Canada, Australia and New Zealand, due January 27, 2027 for undergraduates; you owe a year of federal service after. Boren Scholarship.
  • Gilman. For Pell Grant recipients: up to $5,000 to study abroad, plus $3,000 for a critical language. The next deadline is February 25, 2027. Gilman Scholarship.
  • Fulbright. A year teaching English or studying abroad after you graduate; the next application opens in spring 2027. Fulbright.

Model UN, conferences and events

A plan, year by year

  • Freshman. Take PSCI 103 and 104, start a language and stay with it, and join Model UN.
  • Sophomore. Choose the International Politics track, apply for the Critical Language Scholarship or Gilman, and plan a summer or semester abroad.
  • Junior. Apply for Boren, the Rangel summer program, the State Department internship (in October, once you have 60 credits), Hertog Security Studies and PPIA.

Teaching social studies

Political science majors can become middle and high school social studies teachers through Queens College's School of Education, either alongside the major or after graduating. New York City public schools start new teachers at $71,314 with a bachelor's degree and $80,166 with a master's.

At Queens College

  • Alongside your major. The Social Studies 7–12 program adds 24 credits of education courses in Secondary Education and Youth Services (SEYS) to your political science major; most students start in the second semester of sophomore year. You also need 21 credits of history and geography (two U.S. and two non-U.S. courses), one economics course and a 12-credit block in another social science.
  • To student teach. A 3.0 GPA in your major and in your SEYS courses, a B in SEYS 363, 100 hours of fieldwork and a 2.5 overall GPA. Up to 12 graduate credits taken as an undergraduate can count toward the graduate certificate later.
  • After you graduate. The Advanced Certificate (21 credits) is for people with a bachelor's but no education courses, and the MSEd is for people who already hold a certificate.
  • Who to ask. The social studies education advisors in Secondary Education and Youth Services, Powdermaker Hall 150.
  • NYC Men Teach. Support for QC students with 45 or more credits and a 3.0 GPA who want to teach. NYC Men Teach

New York State certification

For the Social Studies 7–12 initial certificate you need a state-registered program and your college's recommendation, passing scores on the Educating All Students test and the Social Studies Content Specialty Test, workshops on child abuse identification and the Dignity for All Students Act (QC also lists the SAVE workshop) and fingerprint clearance. The edTPA has not been required since April 27, 2022. Check the requirements.

Other routes and help paying

  • NYC Teaching Fellows. Its 2027 cohort has no social studies track, but its special education tracks take a bachelor's in any subject. Teaching Fellows
  • Teach For America. Two years teaching in a public school while you work toward certification. Teach For America
  • Loan forgiveness. Social studies teachers can get up to $5,000 of Teacher Loan Forgiveness after five years at a low-income school. Public school teaching also counts toward Public Service Loan Forgiveness, though the same years can't count for both.
  • A fellowship for future teachers. The James Madison Fellowship pays for a master's for future teachers of American history and government.
  • Closed for now. New York's Teacher Loan Forgiveness program and QC's Teacher Opportunity Corps are no longer taking applications, and the federal TEACH Grant isn't offered for social studies at QC.

AI that helps without writing for you

For profiles, prompts, interview practice and what not to do, see Using AI the right way.

CUNY licenses one generative AI tool for all students: Microsoft Copilot Chat. Sign in with your CUNY Login email, not a personal Microsoft account. That way your chats fall under Microsoft's enterprise data protection and aren't used to train its models. Even so, CUNY's AI guidance says not to share sensitive or identifiable information with any AI tool.

  • Read a posting. Paste in a job or program description and ask for a checklist of what it requires and what you need to prepare.
  • Track deadlines. Ask it to turn your list of programs into a table of deadlines, materials and recommenders, then copy it into Excel.
  • Practice interviews. Ask it to play the interviewer for a specific internship, one question at a time. Answer out loud, then ask for feedback.
  • Prepare for a conversation. Ask it to summarize an organization's recent work before an interview or networking call, then check those facts on the organization's own site.
  • Job-search apps. Apps like Jobright match you to postings and track your applications. Most have a free plan, and many software companies give students a discount: sign up with your CUNY email and look for student pricing before you pay.

Don't let it write your applications. The rule from Statements, help and AI applies here too. Some programs set limits: MIT Blueprint Labs caps code samples at 50% AI-assisted. Many job tools, including LinkedIn Premium, offer to write cover letters or "tailor" your résumé for you; skip those features.

Using AI the right way

AI is a good helper in a job search: it can sharpen your profiles, find openings, check your grammar and quiz you before an interview. It is a bad ghostwriter. Readers notice AI-written applications, and some programs disqualify them.

Where it helps

  • Your LinkedIn and Handshake profiles. Paste in your headline and About section and ask what is vague or missing, then fix it in your own words. LinkedIn's built-in writing help needs Premium; Microsoft's free module shows how to do it with Copilot. Handshake has its own AI features, and a complete profile shows up in more employer searches.
  • Finding jobs. Describe the job you want in plain words in Handshake or LinkedIn's AI job search, and ask AI which job titles to search for.
  • Proofreading. Give it your draft and ask it to point out grammar mistakes, unclear sentences and weak verbs, not to rewrite it. Harvard's career office shows how.
  • Fixing your résumé. Paste a job posting and your résumé and ask which skills the posting wants that you haven't shown, then add only what's true. Video: Tailor your résumé using Gemini (Google, 5 minutes).
  • Practicing interviews. Ask it to play the interviewer, one question at a time, and to give feedback on each answer. Videos: Practice interviewing with Gemini Live (Google, 3 minutes) and Prepare for a job interview with AI (UNLV Career Services, 12 minutes). Free practice tools: Interviews by AI (3 questions a month) and Yoodli (5 practice rounds). For a real person, book a mock interview at the career center.

Not sure what you want to do?

Tell it who you are and what you like, and it can point you to jobs you haven't heard of. Then check what it says against real sources, like O*NET, the career center and people who do the job. Columbia's career center has more ideas.

Prompts to try

I'm a [year] political science major at Queens College in New York. I like [classes or topics], I'm good at [skills], and I care about [issues]. I don't know what job I want. Suggest 10 entry-level job titles that fit me, what each one does day to day, and what I'd need to get hired.

For [job title], what does a normal day look like, what do people like and dislike about it, and what could I do this semester to find out if it's for me?

Here is a job posting: [paste]. Here is my experience: [paste]. What am I missing, and how could I build it in the next six months?

Act as an interviewer for [job] at [organization]. Ask me one question at a time, wait for my answer, then tell me what was strong and what to improve.

Don't let it write for you. In a Robert Half survey (March 2026), 67% of HR leaders said AI-generated applications were slowing hiring. Some applications say it outright: the Commonpoint Data & Insights Fellowship asks for answers in your own words and may disqualify ones that look AI-generated, and 45% of the law schools in a Kaplan survey ban using AI to write the admissions essay. Skip tools that feed you answers during a real interview; employers prohibit them.

Remember: you can coast through a degree using AI all through college, and maybe even graduate, but no one will hire you if you're not competent or useful.

Undocumented and DACA students

Undocumented students and DACA recipients go on to graduate school, law school and good jobs, and many programs on this site are open to them. Here is where to get help at Queens College and CUNY, how to pay for school and what the rules are now.

Help at Queens College and CUNY

  • Immigrant Student Support Initiative. Queens College's office for immigrant students "regardless of their immigration status." King Hall 213, 718-997-3990, ImmigrantSupport@qc.cuny.edu; book through QC Navigate (care unit "Student Affairs," tag "Immigrant Support"). Its pages cover money, scholarships, work and legal help. Immigrant Student Support
  • CUNY Citizenship Now! Free immigration legal help for any New Yorker: citizenship, DACA renewals, TPS and fee waivers. The Queens College center is in the Student Union, Room 100, by appointment (646-347-4774), or call 646-664-9400. Citizenship Now!
  • Quick legal questions. The CUNY Immigration Assistance Project offers short consultations with an attorney for current CUNY students. New York State's Office for New Americans has a hotline: 1-800-566-7636.

DACA right now

U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services still accepts and processes DACA renewals and work permits, and current grants stay valid until they expire, but it "will not process initial DACA requests at this time." Its page was last updated in January 2025. Talk to Citizenship Now! before you file anything. uscis.gov/DACA

Paying for school

  • Federal aid. Undocumented students and DACA recipients can't get federal grants or loans, so state aid and private scholarships matter more.
  • The New York State DREAM Act. Opens up state aid like TAP and the Excelsior Scholarship if you went to a New York high school for two or more years and graduated, earned a New York high school equivalency diploma, or pay in-state tuition at CUNY or SUNY (with time limits after your diploma). It doesn't ask applicants without status for a home address or financial records, and you only establish eligibility once. NYS DREAM Act
  • Scholarships. TheDream.US (up to $33,000 for college); the Paul & Daisy Soros Fellowships for graduate school, which accept DACA recipients (up to $25,000 a year plus half of tuition; due October 29, 2026); and the Hispanic Scholarship Fund, which accepts DACA recipients and graduate students.
  • Graduate school loans. TheDream.US runs graduate loan programs, including a private loan open to graduates with DACA or TPS.
  • Lists to check. Immigrants Rising's scholarship list (no proof of citizenship needed) and My Undocumented Life's graduate school funding. On this site, see every program open to DACA students.

Working

  • Without work authorization. You can still earn money as an independent contractor or by starting a business. An ITIN lets you file taxes and get paid as a contractor, though it does not authorize work or give you status. See QC's work opportunities page and Immigrants Rising.
  • Paid projects. TheDream.US scholars at QC can do paid micro-internships through Parker Dewey, about $20 an hour and up to $4,000 a year, with an SSN or ITIN and a 2.5 GPA. QC's internship stipend is open to undocumented students with ITINs.

Law school and the bar

  • New York admits DACA recipients as lawyers. In Matter of Vargas (2015), a New York appeals court held that "undocumented immigrants who have been granted DACA relief... may be admitted to the practice of law" if they meet the other standards. The decision doesn't address applicants without DACA.
  • LSAT fee waivers. DACA recipients and applicants, TPS holders and affirmative asylum seekers can ask LSAC for a fee waiver. See LSAT and fees.
  • A guide to read. Immigrants Rising's Law School Resource Guide (June 2024). Its Pre-Law Fund ended in 2025.

Rules for undocumented and DACA students change often. Check with the Immigrant Student Support Initiative or Citizenship Now! before you apply or file anything.

Working in politics and campaigns

Campaigns, parties and voter groups take on students every year, and New York has an election every November. You can start as a volunteer, a poll worker or an intern, whatever your party, or if you don't have one.

What campaign staff do

A common first campaign job is field organizer. In the New York State Democratic Committee's 2026 posting, organizers recruited and trained volunteers, knocked on doors, ran phone banks and community events, and tracked daily goals for reaching voters. No experience was needed, but you needed a phone and a car and had to be ready to move, and it paid $5,525 a month. The Republican National Committee trains organizers through its Republican Leadership Initiative.

When campaigns hire

Campaign jobs last for one election, so they're posted in the months before it and end soon after. Any campaign will take volunteers, which is a good way to meet the staff. For House races, the parties collect résumés: the NRCC's campaign jobs bank for Republicans and the New York State Democratic Committee's jobs page for Democrats in New York.

New York's next elections

State law puts primaries on the fourth Tuesday in June and general elections on the Tuesday after the first Monday in November. That makes the next ones June 22 and November 2, 2027, then June 27 and November 7, 2028; 2028 also has a presidential primary on a date not set yet. Early voting runs from ten days to two days before each election. Confirm dates with the State Board of Elections, and see the city Board of Elections' guide to New York City elections.

Paid election work in New York City

  • Poll worker. The city Board of Elections pays election inspectors $250 a day plus $100 for passing the training, and interpreters up to $250 a day plus $25; you can earn up to $2,750 for ten days. Inspectors must be registered Democrats or Republicans. Interpreters need to be 18, live in the city and be citizens or permanent residents, and Queens needs Chinese, Korean, Hindi and Bengali speakers as well as Spanish. Poll worker jobs
  • NYC Votes. The city's nonpartisan Campaign Finance Board hires part-time community education interns to run voter workshops and registration drives, at $17.85 to $21.40 an hour.
  • Elected officials. Internships with the City Council, the State Senate and Assembly in Albany and Congress, and CUNY's Rogowsky program, which includes a Model State Senate in Albany for students QC nominates.

Party and nonpartisan internships

Presenting and publishing research

Presenting a paper or poster shows graduate programs that you can do research, and it gives you a reason to turn a good class paper into a great one. Start with your best paper, revise it with the professor who assigned it, and send it out.

Get a paper ready

  • Pick one paper. Your strongest research paper, usually from a seminar or an honors thesis. Ask the professor to read a revision; some conferences require a faculty sponsor anyway.
  • Write an abstract. Usually 150 to 500 words, depending on the call: your question, how you answered it and what you found.
  • List it. Put presentations and publications on your CV. See the sample résumé and CV.
