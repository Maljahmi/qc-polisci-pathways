    Choosing a graduate degree

    A political science degree leads to several kinds of graduate programs. Open one to see what it is and what it takes. Not sure yet? Most of the programs on this site exist to help you find out.

    PhDResearch and teaching. Funded, 5–6 years

    What a PhD is

    Original research and teaching. A PhD prepares you to be a professor, or to do research at think tanks, in government and in tech. Political science PhD programs pay you.

    Length
    5–6 years
    Who pays
    The school: tuition plus a stipend. 2026–27: Princeton $52,680, Duke $43,775, CUNY Graduate Center $31,141
    Pick it if
    You want answering questions with data or theory to be your career
    Apply
    Fall of senior year or later. Most deadlines fall Dec 1–17; Columbia's is Nov 25. See Applying
    A political science PhD can also lead to data science.

    PhD students train in statistics, causal inference, survey design and coding, and some go straight into industry. Michigan's placement records include a research scientist on Meta's demography and survey science team, a director of data science at Civiqs and a lead data scientist at Cox Automotive. MIT's include a data scientist at NORC. Choosing methodology as a minor field keeps that door open.

    A master's is not required for a PhD.

    Most political science PhD students are admitted straight from a BA or after a paid research job. A paid year or two first is normal; the standard route is a predoc, a full-time research assistant job.

    Where to look.

    predoc.org is the main job board; filter by political economy or public policy. Listings go stale, so confirm on the lab's own page. For a pay benchmark, Stanford's SIEPR economics predoc posted $64,260 a year.

    Find your subfield

    You don't have to be a "methods person" to get a political science PhD. You apply to study one subfield, only some subfields are math-heavy, and most programs teach statistics from the start.

    The five core subfields

    Mixedoften quantitative

    American politics

    Also called American government and politics

    U.S. institutions such as Congress, the presidency and the courts, plus elections, public opinion and political behavior.

    Look for
    No special prerequisites are listed. Expect statistics: Harvard's math prefresher notes it is hard to take American politics courses without meeting statistical or game-theoretic models
    Mixedqualitative and quantitative

    Comparative politics

    Compares politics across countries and regions: democracy, development, civil war, institutions and identity.

    Look for
    A foreign language and knowledge of a region. Rutgers tests majors in the language they need, and Michigan advises starting languages before graduate school
    Research
    Berkeley PhD students usually spend at least a year doing fieldwork
    Mixeddepends on approach

    International relations

    Also called international relations and world politics

    War and security, trade, international law and organizations, and why countries cooperate or fight.

    Look for
    Methods vary: historical, statistical and game-theoretic work all appear. No IR-specific prerequisites are listed
    Lightlittle math

    Political theory

    Also called political thought, or normative political theory and philosophy

    Ideas of justice, legitimacy, democracy and power, read through the history of political thought and contemporary philosophy.

    Look for
    Reading in philosophy, history or classics, and often a foreign language: UVA requires one for theory majors
    Methods
    Duke's methods requirements don't apply to theory students, Harvard lets them skip its methods course, and Berkeley weighs math less for theory applicants
    Heavyreal math

    Methodology and formal theory

    Also called research methods, quantitative methodology, or formal and quantitative analysis

    Methodologists build the statistical and research-design tools other political scientists use. Formal theorists use game-theory models of strategic choices.

    Look for
    Math and statistics. Duke's methodology field expects competency in both, and Michigan asks methods majors for math coursework
    Tip
    Many students take methods as a minor next to a substantive subfield

    You don't have to arrive methods-heavy

    • Math camps. Harvard, Stanford, Michigan, Princeton and UC San Diego run summer math courses for incoming PhD students. At Harvard, almost every first-year student takes it.
    • Methods from scratch. Stanford says you don't need advanced quantitative courses to be admitted, and its first-year sequence assumes no background in statistics or advanced math.
    • Language or methods. At the CUNY Graduate Center, UCLA, Yale and Columbia, a research-tools requirement can be met with a foreign language or with methods courses.
    • Your topic can change. Michigan notes that students often change focus once coursework starts. The statement of purpose is not a contract.
    Be honest with yourself about math.

    Quantitative skills still count. Most top programs require the GRE again, and Stanford reports admitted students typically score 163 or higher on the quantitative section. Stanford also requires two methods courses of every PhD student. Taking PSCI 200 and an intro statistics course, such as PSYCH 107 or DATA 205, helps whichever subfield you choose.

    Other fields at some schools

    Some departments add fields beyond the core five. Check whether a field can be your main field or only a minor.

    • Public policy. CUNY Graduate Center (Public Policy, with areas like health, education, housing and urban development), UC Berkeley (Public Policy & Organization), UT Austin, Texas A&M (Public Policy / Public Administration), Syracuse (public administration and policy) and Michigan (a student-designed Public Policy and Administration minor).
    • Law and courts. Princeton and Rutgers (Public Law), Michigan (Law, Courts, and Politics), UC Berkeley (Public Law & Jurisprudence), Washington and UT Austin, which also has a combined JD/PhD.
    • Race, ethnicity and gender. UCLA (Race, Ethnicity & Politics), Rutgers (Women and Politics), UC Berkeley, Washington and Ohio State (Race, Ethnicity, and Gender).
    • Political psychology and behavior. Stony Brook, Ohio State and Minnesota (as a minor).
    • Political economy and formal theory. Rochester (formal political theory; every student takes it or methodology), Yale, NYU and UChicago, which runs a separate Political Economy PhD with its public policy school. Expect heavy math.
    • Organized by theme. Duke has no American, comparative or international relations fields. Its six fields are Political Behavior & Identities, Political Institutions, Political Economy, Security, Peace & Conflict, Political Methodology, and Normative Political Theory & Philosophy.

    PredocA paid research job before a PhD. Any major, 1–2 years

    What a predoc is

    A predoc is a full-time, paid research job with a professor or research center, usually for one or two years before a PhD. You clean data, run analyses and see how research gets made. Economics departments hire the most predocs, but political science, sociology and public policy centers hire them too, and you don't need to major in economics.

    Length
    1–2 years, usually starting in July
    Pay
    Posted salaries run about $59,500 to $69,300 a year (Stanford GSB, the Stanford King Center, SIEPR and MIT); Stanford IRiSS pays $32.45 an hour
    Any major
    Yale ISPS "very strongly encourage[s]" applicants who didn't major in political science, Opportunity Insights says applicants "need not be Economics majors," and the Stanford King Center welcomes "all disciplines"
    When to apply
    The fall of senior year. Many economics predocs close between August and October (in 2026: Opportunity Insights Oct 1, SIEPR Oct 8, Stanford GSB Oct 16; Yale's Tobin Center opens its rolling application Oct 5). Political science ones, such as Yale ISPS and Stanford IRiSS, hire on a rolling basis or in spring
    Where to look
    predoc.org, filtered by field, and the research centers in the list below

    Consider an economics minor.

    Most predocs are in economics, and economics coursework helps you qualify. That doesn't mean you have to do an economics PhD: a predoc is strong research training in general, and some are in finance and behavioral research. Stanford GSB's Research Fellows, for example, work across finance, organizational behavior and political economics. See the economics minor.

    The writing sample

    Have an empirical paper finished by the summer before your senior year. It doesn't have to be long: 12 to 15 pages is appropriate. Several postings ask for an excerpt of about 5 pages, so know which section you would send.

    • What makes it empirical. A clear question, real data, a method (at least a regression), results in tables or figures, and an honest section on limitations.
    • Where it can come from. A paper for a writing-intensive seminar, DATA 334, ECON 390 or a chapter of your honors thesis. Ask the professor to read a draft.
    • Data you can use. ICPSR (Queens College is a member), the American National Election Studies, the Cooperative Election Study, IPUMS census and survey data, and NYC Open Data.
    • Write it yourself. A Harvard Kennedy School posting asked for a single-authored sample, and MIT Blueprint Labs says code samples must be "no more than 50% AI-assisted." Use AI as a proofreader at most (see Statements, help and AI).
    • Keep a code sample too. Seven of the twelve postings ask for a code sample or a data task. Keep the R, Stata or Python script behind your paper clean and commented.

    What an MPP is

    A Master of Public Policy teaches you to analyze and evaluate policy with economics and statistics. It is faster than a PhD, but you usually pay for it.

    Length
    About 2 years
    Cost
    You usually pay. Some schools fund everyone: Princeton SPIA's MPA, a policy degree, covers full tuition for every student
    At CUNY
    Baruch's Marxe School starts an MPP in fall 2027: evening classes, in person and hybrid, 36–39 credits, about 2 years full-time. Tuition is $545 a credit for New York residents, pending CUNY Board approval
    Pick it if
    You want to design policy or judge whether programs work
    Prepare
    Take economics and statistics. For a future MPP or MPA student, the most valuable program is the PPIA Junior Summer Institute
    MPP or MPA?

    Schools use the two labels loosely, so read each program's courses, not just its name. An MPP leans on analysis; an MPA leans on management.

    Start here

    This path covers funded PhDs and policy master's for any Queens College Political Science major who is even curious about graduate school. You don't need to be sure yet. Most of these programs exist to help you find out.

    Three ground rules

    • Good research programs pay you. Almost everything here is free and comes with a stipend, housing, or both. Be skeptical of programs that charge you to "do research."
    • Deadlines move every year. Confirm on the official page before you plan around a date.
    • Apply to more than one. These programs are competitive, and a rejection says little about you.
    Take the methods courses earlyPSCI 200 (Research Skills) and an intro statistics course such as PSYCH 107 or DATA 205. Either one also counts toward the Data Analytics minor.
    Ask for research assistant workNo office posts it. Pick one or two professors whose work you understand, and email what you read, what skill you bring and how many hours you have.
    Get credit for it.

    QC's political science internship courses give credit for urban politics, law and advocacy, and international politics placements. CUNY's Rogowsky Internship Program places juniors and seniors in government for 3–12 credits.

    Undocumented or an international student?

    Emory SOAR says it accepts undocumented and international students. MIT MSRP accepts international students with a visa and work authorization. RBSI, MIT and Michigan also take DACA recipients.

    Get readyTalking to professors, what to pair with your major, methods and skills, notes from RBSI

    Talk to your professors

    Your research doesn't have to be political science. Methods travel across fields, and CUNY faculty in many departments need student help with surveys, data, experiments, fieldwork and archives.

    What research looks like next door

    • Sociology. Surveys and interviews on inequality, immigration, work and neighborhoods. Home of QC's data analytics program.
    • Economics. Labor markets and policy evaluation, often with large administrative datasets and causal inference.
    • Psychology. Experiments on attitudes, prejudice and decision-making, the base of political psychology.
    • Urban studies. Housing, transit, policing and city budgets, often with NYC open data.
    • Anthropology and history. Fieldwork, oral histories and archives.
    • Linguistics. Text as data, and language and identity (see Pair your major).

    Going to office hours

    Office hours are not only for grade questions. Professors expect students to come in and ask for advice, and most of the opportunities on this site started with a conversation like that.

    • Go to office hours or email to ask for 15 minutes. It's normal, even if you've never taken their class.
    • Ask for advice, not a favor: "What would you do in my position?" "Which classes or programs should I look at?" "Who else should I talk to?"
    • Ask about their research, and read one of their papers first.
    • Look outside your department. Sociology, economics, psychology and urban studies faculty take political science students too.
    • Follow up. Send a thank-you, then tell them what you did with their advice. That is how a professor becomes a recommender.
    Sample email

    Subject: Advice on research opportunities, [Your name], [major]

    Dear Professor [Last name],

    I'm a [year] majoring in [major] at Queens College. I read your [paper or project] on [topic], and I'm interested in [one sentence on why].

    Pair your major

    Take a broad range of courses early. Trying history, philosophy, economics or urban studies is how you find the questions you actually want to study, and a second field can make you a stronger applicant.

    Theory, history and cities

    Philosophy

    Minor (18 credits) or second major

    Arguments about justice, rights and legitimacy, the core of political theory. The minor includes two history-of-philosophy courses (PHIL 140–148). Best for students drawn to political theory.

    History

    Minor or second major

    Primary sources, archives and long-run change. Useful for political theory, comparative politics and the history of American institutions.

    Urban Studies

    Major, or a BA and MA in five years

    City politics, housing, policing and budgets, often with NYC data. The Accelerated MA pairs a BA in Urban Studies with an MA in Urban Affairs in five years, and up to 12 graduate credits count toward both. It needs a 3.0 GPA overall and in the major; majors in other social sciences are considered case by case. Apply at about 45–75 credits. Best for city government and MPA programs.

    Sociology

    Double major

    Survey research and theory; home of Data Analytics. It unlocks the 4+1 BA/MA in data analytics and Mellon Mays, neither of which takes political science alone.

    Data, economics and business

    A quantitative credential helps for policy, campaign, government and research jobs. The Bureau of Labor Statistics projects data scientist jobs to grow 35% from 2025 to 2035 (BLS).

    Data Analytics

    Minor (21 credits), in Sociology

    The easiest add-on for most political science majors, built for students with no background. Courses: DATA 205 (or PSYCH 107 or ECON 249), 212W, 235, 306, 333 and 334 (list). DATA 205, 306 and 333 teach R. Best for analyst and research assistant jobs, predocs and MPP programs.

    Math: Data Science and Statistics

    BA, as a second major

    Statements, help and AI

    Pick programs that fit, and get help with your statement.

    Read what each program focuses on and make sure it matches what you want to study. Then ask your professors and Rebecca Baron, QC's Advisor for Scholarships and Fellowships in the Office of Honors and Scholarships, to help with your statements of purpose. They are here to help. Don't lose out on an opportunity because you were scared to ask. You're in college to learn, so learn.

    Don't let AI write your application.

    AI is useful for some tasks, like building this website. But professors read the applications to these programs, and they will know if AI wrote yours. Use it as a proofreader instead: give it the prompt and your own draft, and ask where you could improve or show more of yourself. It's a machine, and it doesn't know you better than you know yourself. Before you submit, have peers and professors read your writing too.

    Recommendation letters

    Most programs ask for two or three letters. PhD programs want them from professors. For law school, MPA and MPP programs and jobs, a supervisor from a job or internship can write one too.

    Not close with any professors or employers?

    Most students aren't at first. Professors write letters all the time, and they say yes when you make it easy for them.

    • Pick a class you did well in, ideally one where you got a B or above and participated.
    • Ask to meet. Look up their office hours, or email to ask when you could meet on Zoom.
    • Ask 6–8 weeks before the deadline, not the week of.
    • Send everything in one email: your résumé, a draft of your statement and your list of programs with deadlines.
    • Remind and thank. Send a short reminder a week before the deadline, and tell them where you got in.

    Using AI the right way

    AI is a good helper in a job search: it can sharpen your profiles, find openings, check your grammar and quiz you before an interview. It is a bad ghostwriter. Readers notice AI-written applications, and some programs disqualify them.

    Where it helps

    • Your LinkedIn and Handshake profiles. Paste in your headline and About section and ask what is vague or missing, then fix it in your own words. LinkedIn's built-in writing help needs Premium; Microsoft's free module shows how to do it with Copilot. Handshake has its own AI features, and a complete profile shows up in more employer searches.
    • Finding jobs. Describe the job you want in plain words in Handshake or LinkedIn's AI job search, and ask AI which job titles to search for.
    • Proofreading. Give it your draft and ask it to point out grammar mistakes, unclear sentences and weak verbs, not to rewrite it. Harvard's career office shows how.
    • Fixing your résumé. Paste a job posting and your résumé and ask which skills the posting wants that you haven't shown, then add only what's true. Video: Tailor your résumé using Gemini (Google, 5 minutes).
    • Practicing interviews. Ask it to play the interviewer, one question at a time, and to give feedback on each answer. Videos: Practice interviewing with Gemini Live (Google, 3 minutes) and Prepare for a job interview with AI (UNLV Career Services, 12 minutes). Free practice tools: Interviews by AI (3 questions a month) and Yoodli (5 practice rounds). For a real person, book a mock interview at the career center.

    Not sure what you want to do?

    Tell it who you are and what you like, and it can point you to jobs you haven't heard of. Then check what it says against real sources, like O*NET, the career center and people who do the job. Columbia's career center has more ideas.

    Prompts to try

    I'm a [year] political science major at Queens College in New York. I like [classes or topics], I'm good at [skills], and I care about [issues]. I don't know what job I want. Suggest 10 entry-level job titles that fit me, what each one does day to day, and what I'd need to get hired.

    For [job title], what does a normal day look like, what do people like and dislike about it, and what could I do this semester to find out if it's for me?

    Here is a job posting: [paste]. Here is my experience: [paste]. What am I missing, and how could I build it in the next six months?

    Act as an interviewer for [job] at [organization]. Ask me one question at a time, wait for my answer, then tell me what was strong and what to improve.

    Don't let it write for you.

    In a Robert Half survey (March 2026), 67% of HR leaders said AI-generated applications were slowing hiring. Some applications say it outright: the Commonpoint Data & Insights Fellowship asks for answers in your own words and may disqualify ones that look AI-generated, and 45% of the law schools in a Kaplan survey ban using AI to write the admissions essay. Skip tools that feed you answers during a real interview; employers prohibit them.

    Remember: you can coast through a degree using AI all through college, and maybe even graduate, but no one will hire you if you're not competent or useful.

    Building a network

    Many jobs and internships come through people you know, and you're in New York City, which has more networking events than you could ever go to.

    • Ask your professors. Tell them the field you're curious about, and ask whether they know anyone you could talk to.
    • Use the career center. The Center for Career Engagement and Internships runs workshops and events and a career and internship fair where employers recruit.
    • Go to events. Look for talks, panels and mixers at CUNY, nonprofits, think tanks and city agencies; many are free. Introduce yourself, ask one good question, and follow up by email.

    This is all general advice, so don't feel pressured. I know it's overwhelming. But if you put in your best effort and explore what you really like, you can find a job in that sector, whatever you want it to be, or get into law school or graduate school. Just take your time and put effort into your classwork, your applications and networking. Don't be afraid: people are usually nice and will help you. You just have to make it easy for them!

    What law school takes

    Admissions weigh two numbers most, your LSAT score and your GPA, then your personal statement, letters and résumé. Your major does not need to be pre-law or political science. Not sure what kind of law? See Fields of law.

    One application cycle (LSAC's steps)

    1. Spring before you applyRequest the LSAC fee waiver if you might qualify, at least 6 months before your first LSAT.
    2. June or September–OctoberTake the LSAT. Many schools want a score by November or December.
    3. SummerDraft the personal statement; ask 2–3 professors or supervisors for letters.
    4. 6–8 weeks before your first deadlineRegister for CAS, which sends your transcripts and letters to every school.
    5. September–DecemberSubmit applications. Earlier is better at schools that admit on a rolling basis.
    6. Winter–springDecisions and scholarship offers. QC's pre-law advisor helps you compare and negotiate.

    The LSAT today

    Since August 2024 there are no logic games: two scored Logical Reasoning sections, one scored Reading Comprehension section and one unscored section, scored 120–180, plus a separate Argumentative Writing sample (LSAC). Many schools also accept the GRE.

    Fields of law

    Law is many different jobs. You don't have to pick a field before law school, but knowing them helps you choose internships and explain your interest in your personal statement. For a paragraph on each, see LSAC's Fields of Law; for more depth, read the guide linked under each field below. Most were written by law school career offices.

    Criminal lawProsecutors charge and try crimes for the government; defense lawyers, often public defenders, represent the people accused. Read: UCLA Law's guides to public defense and prosecution. Try it: the Queens DA or the city's defender offices.
    Civil rights and civil libertiesLawyers who use lawsuits, policy advocacy and government enforcement to protect equal treatment, voting, speech and other rights. Read: Civil Rights/Civil Liberties (Georgetown Law). Try it: the ACLU, LatinoJustice LAWbound or the Justice Department's Civil Rights Division.
    Immigration lawLawyers who help people get visas, green cards, citizenship or asylum, and defend people facing deportation. A second language helps. Read: Immigration Law Career Guide (Harvard Law). Try it: Immigrant Justice Corps after college.
    Public interest and legal aidNonprofit lawyers who represent low-income clients for free in problems like evictions, debt, public benefits and consumer fraud. Read: Legal Services Career Guide (Harvard Law, 2013). Try it: the Hope Reichbach Fellowship at a Brooklyn nonprofit.
    GovernmentCity, state and federal lawyers who advise agencies, enforce and defend laws in court, and draft legislation and regulations. Read: State and Local Government Career Guide (Harvard Law), which profiles the NYC Law Department. Try it: the State Attorney General or the Mayor's Office.
    Corporate and business lawLawyers who help companies form, raise money, merge and make deals, mostly drafting and negotiating contracts rather than going to court. Most start at large firms. Read: Careers in Transactional Law (Georgetown Law, 2021). Try it: the SEO Law Fellowship.
    Civil litigationLawyers who handle non-criminal lawsuits between people, companies or governments, from gathering evidence through settlement, trial and appeal. Read: Civil Litigation (University of Baltimore Law). Try it: a judge's chambers through the Sotomayor internship or the state courts.
    International law and human rightsLawyers working on the rules between countries and on human rights, for governments, the UN, international courts, nonprofits or global firms. Read: Careers in Public International Law and Human Rights (UCLA Law, 2019). Try it: the Council on Foreign Relations or, after college, the Carnegie Junior Fellows.
    Environmental and energy lawLawyers working on pollution, climate, land, water and energy rules for agencies, advocacy groups, companies or utilities. Read: Different Career Paths in Environmental Law (American Bar Association, 2020). Try it: the Udall Scholarship.
    Labor and employment lawLawyers who handle unions, wages, discrimination and firing, for workers, unions, employers or agencies like the NLRB and EEOC. Read: Employment/Labor Law (Georgetown Law).
    Family lawLawyers who handle divorce, custody, child support, adoption, domestic violence and child-welfare cases. Read: Constructing a Legal Education for a Career in Family Law (NYU Law), which names New York City employers. Try it: the city's defender offices, several of which have family defense practices.
    Intellectual property, technology and privacyLawyers who protect inventions, brands and creative work, and advise on technology, data privacy and cybersecurity. Patent work needs a science or engineering background; trademark, copyright and privacy work doesn't. Read: Intellectual Property Law (Georgetown Law).
    Health lawLawyers who advise hospitals, insurers, drug companies, patients and agencies on health regulation, fraud, privacy and access to care. Read: Health Law (Georgetown Law).
    Tax lawLawyers who plan deals around tax rules and handle audits and disputes, at firms, accounting firms, companies or the government. Read: An Introduction to Tax Careers for J.D.s (American Bar Association, 2019).
    Election law and voting rightsLawyers who work on voting rights, redistricting, campaign finance and how elections are run, for campaigns, parties, nonprofits and election agencies. Close to political science. Read: Polling Election Lawyers (Harvard Law's Center on the Legal Profession, 2022). Try it: the Brennan Center or Emgage in Queens.

    Research as a pre-law student

    Research isn't required for law school, but it builds what admissions reads for: careful writing and a professor who knows your work.

    The LSAC fee waiver

    Based on income and assets. Income limits run from 150% to 300% of the federal poverty guidelines, depending on the tier and whether your parents' income counts. Tier 1 covers 2 LSATs with Writing and Score Preview, CAS, 6 law school reports and LawHub Advantage; Tier 2 covers 1 LSAT, CAS, 3 reports and LawHub Advantage. The LSAT and CAS benefits last two years; LawHub Advantage lasts one year. US citizens, permanent residents, DACA recipients, asylum seekers and TPS holders are eligible. Apply, and ask each law school whether it waives its own fee too.

    A paid job between college and law school is the norm at top schools, and the work you do can become the story in your personal statement.
